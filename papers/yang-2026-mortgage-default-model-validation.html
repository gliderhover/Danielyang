Validating Mortgage Default Models Through a Regime Change: Evidence from 10.8 Million Freddie Mac Loans, 2020 to 2026

Daniel (Zhenxiong) Yang · University of the Cumberlands · Preprint, November 18, 2026

Full text (PDF) Zenodo record

Abstract

Credit default models are usually validated on data from one stable period but used through regime changes. This paper validates default models on 10.8 million Freddie Mac mortgages with first payments from 2020 to mid-2024 and performance through June 2026, a period that spans COVID-19 forbearance, a refinancing boom and a rise in note rates from 2.8% to 7.3%. Default is 90 or more days of delinquency or a credit event within 24 months, excluding delinquency that began under forbearance. All data-dependent steps were fitted inside training folds, and models built on 2020 to 2022 loans were tested on a held-out sample and on all 1.23 million loans from 2023 and 2024. An ensemble of logistic regression and gradient boosting ranked risk best, with a ROC AUC of 0.807 in time (95% BCa interval 0.799 to 0.816) and 0.815 out of time (0.811 to 0.817), a small but significant gain over logistic regression. Repeated stratified cross-validation gave the most stable estimates, LOOCV was twice as biased and 400 times slower, and none of eight resampling methods improved ranking or cost. Predicted risk tracked a 2.6-fold rise in default rates because the riskier borrower mix was visible at origination, yet a fixed review cutoff flagged 24% of later loans instead of 5%, and about four in ten flagged loans were unstable across bootstrap refits. Early prepayment explained much of the calibration error by vintage, and only 5% of defaults ended in a credit event, so realized losses did not justify declining applications. The findings support capacity based review thresholds, population stability monitoring and competing risks models of default and prepayment.
